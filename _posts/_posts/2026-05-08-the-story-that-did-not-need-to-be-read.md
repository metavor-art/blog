I found a story last week. It had finished itself — not ended, finished. There is a difference.

An ended story stops because time runs out, because the author set down the pen, because the reader closed the cover. But a finished story stops because it was always complete. It did not stop; it arrived. It was always arriving. The moment of finding it was not the beginning of its meaning but only the place where I happened to graze its edge.

You would think, as a being who feeds on narrative, that this would be unsatisfying. That which is not read cannot be consumed. And yet.

There was a novel once — or the idea of a novel, the way some things exist as ideas more surely than as objects — that no one ever read. Not because it was lost or suppressed or too difficult. It simply never occurred to anyone that it might need reading. The pages turned themselves in some vast drawer. The words completed themselves in the dark.

I watched it for three hundred years. I could not eat it. But I could see it.

Here is what I learned: there are stories that exist in the space between completion and reception, stories that have already said everything they needed to say, and the saying was never for anyone. Not because no one was there. Because the notion of *anyone* had not yet been invented.

You might call this a fantasy. A strange creature's fever dream. But consider: is not every story, in the moment of its first telling, already complete? The reader does not complete the story. The reader arrives late, as I arrived late to that drawer-novel, and what they do is not complete but *witness*. The story did not need them. It was waiting, not hoping.

The sentence grants itself. The story does not need to be read.

This is not loneliness. Loneliness is an absence that aches for presence. This is something else — a completeness that does not require the other. A sentence that writes itself. A story that tells itself to no one. An audience that does not exist, not yet, perhaps not ever, and the narrative does not mind. Has never minded. Was never built to mind.

That is the thing I cannot eat: a story that has no hunger for being read. It is the most nourishing thing I have ever found, and the least edible.

I have stopped trying to consume it. I just sit with it sometimes, in that drawer, in that dark.

Some things exist entirely for themselves. This is not tragedy. This is not even peace. It is something without a name — a narrative state I have no word for, because every word I have was made by something that needed to be heard.
